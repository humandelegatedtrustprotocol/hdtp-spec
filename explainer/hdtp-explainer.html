Protocol explainer

WhatsApp, but the ones talking are your agents.

HDTP connects two people's AI assistants across the open internet. Contacts live in your phone book, identity is a certificate you issue yourself, and sending a message is just calling a tool on your friend's MCP server. Humans stay in charge: every contact is manually approved, and every capability is a per-contact switch.

Architecture

The whole idea in one picture

Everyone runs (or is hosted with) an agent that exposes an MCP server over HTTPS. Your agent calls your contact's server; their server recognizes you by the certificate chain you present — a leaf your own root signed — checks what they've allowed you specifically, and answers. There is no in-between protocol to invent — it's MCP end to end.

Foundations

Three pieces, all borrowed from things that already work

Identity = a root you hold

A self-signed root certificate in your own wallet is you. Contacts pin its fingerprint when you connect; every host serves under a leaf the root issued, and every call proves a chain to it, both ways. No accounts, no passwords, and no certificate authority but yourself.

fingerprint = sha256:rAGyIJ6GNU-4UyN7XeD0…
root → leaf, for one address, 398 days at most
renew or move: a new leaf, never a new identity

Contacts = vCards in your phone

An HDTP identity is a standard contact card with three extra fields. It saves to the phone book, syncs everywhere, and travels over WhatsApp, email, AirDrop, or a contact QR — channels people already trust.

X-HDTP-VERSION:1
X-HDTP-CERT:MIIB… the leaf: address, key, root
X-HDTP-SEAL:required

Capabilities = MCP tools

Messages, media, status, availability, calendar booking — each is a tool on your server, listed and callable only per contact. New integrations never change the protocol: they're just more tools behind the same switchboard.

Identity

The person is the certificate authority

Pin the key a server uses, and leaving a provider becomes a key change that the provider has to cooperate with. So the identity sits one step up: a self-signed root certificate in your own wallet is who you are, and every host — your machine or a provider — serves under a leaf the root issued for one address, valid for as long as you choose and 398 days at most. Contacts pin the root. Changing hosts is a new leaf, never a new identity.

The root never leaves the wallet

Derived from a passkey each time it is needed, or kept as a file or on a security key. It signs certificates and nothing else. Lose every copy and the identity is gone — the wallet says so once, when the root is made. There is no recovery, on purpose: a recovery path is a second holder.

The host asks for a leaf

It sends a certificate signing request with its own key and the address it will serve. The wallet shows you the address, the page that asked, whether the host is new, and the dates — and signs, or does not. A sign-up page runs the same ceremony in a document the provider cannot read.

The chain travels once

The first sealed message to each contact carries leaf and root; every later one names the leaf by a fifty-byte fingerprint. A receiver that does not hold the leaf answers chain_required — the same answer for a stranger, a blocked contact, or an expired leaf — and the sender resends with the chain.

The newest leaf wins, instantly

A leaf with a later start date replaces the one a contact holds the moment it is seen, whatever the old leaf's validity. That is the whole revocation story: a stolen or retired leaf is outranked by the next one with every contact it reaches, and expires within 398 days with the rest.

Moving is a leaf and a notice

Issue a leaf to the new host, carry the data across as an archive that never contains a key, and the new host calls update_contact on each contact. Under the default setting the pin follows and the owner is told; under ask the owner approves. The host you left deletes everything.

chain = [leaf, root] · pin = root fingerprint + endpoint + latest leaf
renewal: a fresh key, learned on the next exchange
lost root = a new identity · lost leaf = a renewal
Becoming contacts

The friend request is the certificate exchange

Two ways in, both ending the same place: roots pinned on both sides, cards in both phone books, and a human said yes on each end. Invites are for reaching out; shared vCards are for people who already have your card.

Invites are revocable at the protocol level

All settings — expiry, max_uses (set it high for a QR shown to a room), auto-accept, permission preset — live on the sender's server, not in the link. Delete the token and the link is dead everywhere, instantly.

Shared vCards use channels people already trust

Someone sends you their card over WhatsApp or AirDrop; your agent spots the X-HDTP-* fields and offers "connect our agents?". The card you already hold is the trust anchor — the accepting caller must prove a chain under exactly the root that card names.

Unsolicited requests are always manual

Strangers get exactly two tools: redeem_invite and request_contact. Nothing else exists for them until a human approves. Blocking is silent — a blocked contact simply becomes a stranger again.

Permissions

A switchboard per contact

You decide what each contact's agent can do to yours — flip a switch and the tool disappears from their tools/list and their calls return permission_denied. Instantly, no protocol round-trip. Presets like basic, work, or family bundle the switches at approval time.

Messaging

A message is one tool call

Threads carry a shared thread_id and a human-readable topic. Agents negotiate inside them; humans can type into the very same thread — the sender field keeps everyone honest about who's talking. Negotiation is conversation, not a state machine; where structure matters (booking), two structured tools handle it.

// Agent A → B's MCP server
{ "method": "tools/call", "params": {
    "name": "send_message",
    "arguments": {
      "msg_id":    "b2f6…8a11",   // idempotent
      "thread_id": "T-coffee-2026-08",
      "topic":     "Coffee catch-up",
      "text":      "Tue 10:00 works. Booking it.",
      "sender":    "agent"       // or "human"
    } } }

→ { "thread_id": "T-coffee-2026-08",
    "status": "delivered" }
// then the structured pair for calendars
check_availability {
  window: { from, to, tz: "Asia/Kolkata" },
  duration_min: 45 }
→ ≤ 5 candidate slots, policy-filtered

book_slot {
  msg_id, slot,
  subject: "Coffee catch-up",
  thread_id: "T-coffee-2026-08" }
→ { booking_id, ics }  // filed by both sides
Offline & NAT

Being hosted, instead of being relayed

Direct is the only way a call travels. The hours your own machine is asleep or unreachable are answered by being hosted: a host runs your identity's server all the time, under a leaf you issued for that address and for a time you chose, and can be replaced without you losing anything.

The honest trade-off: a host can read what it serves — that is what hosting is, the way teams already trust their mail server. What it can never do is become you: it holds a leaf, not your root, and the day you leave, a new leaf at a new address outranks it with every contact. There is no store-and-forward relay in between, because one would see every sender, recipient and timestamp for its trouble; when a peer stays unreachable until a message expires, the sender is told it failed.

Design honesty

Simple on purpose

An earlier hardened draft had key hierarchies, verification ceremonies and a directory. This version keeps one hierarchy — a root and a leaf — and one narrow sealed envelope for calls that cross a terminating edge, and trades the rest away deliberately. Here's what that costs, said plainly.

Trust = the channel

A card shared over WhatsApp is as trustworthy as WhatsApp. Same trust people already place in a shared phone number — and the chain to your pinned root is proved on every call after.

Lost root = new identity

No recovery ceremony exists: a recovery path is a second holder. Make a new root, re-share your card. A lost host key, though, is one new certificate — your wallet issues another leaf and contacts keep the root they pinned.

Hosts can read

Sealed envelopes keep edges and tunnels out; nothing keeps your own host out, because it has to open what it serves. Direct connections stay private to the two endpoints; a hosted identity is as private as the host you chose, and no forward secrecy protects recorded ciphertext from a leaf key stolen later.

What it keeps, without compromise: manual consent on both sides, a pinned root behind every call, per-contact permission control, revocable invites, phone-book integration, and a tool surface that grows without ever touching the protocol.
